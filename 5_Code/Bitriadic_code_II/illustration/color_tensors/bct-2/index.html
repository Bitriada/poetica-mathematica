BCT-2 · Colors that remember

Every color on every screen you have ever seen is three numbers: how much red, green and blue light. That is enough to look at colors. It is not enough to ask how a color was made. This page is a working demonstration of a different idea — a color that carries its own history: what light went in, what ink bit it, and how the books balance.

1 · Six facts you can poke

No jargon below — just colors, lights, inks and books. Each fact states something true about the model, explains it in plain words, and then lets you make it happen with buttons. Watch the color, the numbers, and the position shift.

Fact 1 — Three lights, three inks

Every color here is built from six controls: three lights (red, blue, green) and three inks (cyan, yellow, magenta). Lights add — pile them up and you get white. Inks subtract — and an ink only does something when there is light for it to eat: cyan ink eats exactly red light, yellow eats blue, magenta eats green.

Try: turn on all three lights (white!). Now add cyan ink — watch it eat exactly the red. Then turn the red light off: with no red to eat, the cyan ink does nothing at all.

Fact 2 — The same grey, three ways

Pair each light with the ink that eats it, and the books balance: as much flowing as bitten. Three different pairs — red+cyan, blue+yellow, green+magenta — and all three land on the same grey. Same to the eye. Different in the books.

Try: click through the three pairs — the swatch never changes, the books always do. Then press “all three lights”: that white is not the same grey — it is a genuinely different color, with no ink in it at all.

Fact 3 — Every color has a mirror

Swap every light for ink and every ink for light, and you get the color’s mirror: whatever flowed is now absorbed, whatever was bright is now dark, and the books flip sign. Screens and printing presses are the same machine run in opposite directions.

Watch the totals: light and ink trade places, the balance number flips from + to − (or − to +). Forty colors live on each side of the mirror; exactly one — the perfect grey — is its own mirror.

Fact 4 — Grey is a balance, not a color

Ask “how grey is this color?” and you get a number: how far the books are from balancing. Slide from all-light to all-ink and you walk the whole ladder — through every degree of one-sidedness, passing through grey, the one point where everything cancels. In all 81 color-states of this model, exactly one balances perfectly: every column half light, half ink.

balance (all-light +15 … grey 0 … all-ink −15): 0

Try: park the slider on 0. One state. Now nudge to +1 or −3 — suddenly several different color-states share the same balance number. Which brings us to Fact 5.

Fact 5 — One number, many colors

The balance number collides. Five different color-states all balance to +3 — the number cannot tell them apart. Any tool that only counts balance is blind to how the color was made. This is the model’s own limit, stated plainly — and it is exactly where the interesting mathematics lives.

+3

Five states, five different colors and histories, one shared number. A color-picker that stores “+3” has already forgotten which of these five you meant.

Fact 6 — The notebook will not write the same memory twice

The ink side of the books is a strange notebook. Write “cyan was absorbed” — fine, it is recorded. Write it again — the note annihilates itself: the same memory, recorded twice, erases. Light, by contrast, just piles up. That asymmetry is the deepest rule in the model (its full name is τ² = 0 — you will meet it in the machine).

The notebook is empty.

Try: press the ink button twice in a row. Then press the light button three times — light accumulates (1, 2, 3…), memory does not. Multiply two ink-notes together and you get exactly nothing: (cyan) × (cyan) = 0.

2 · The Fruition game

A small roguelike built on the six facts. You are given a broken color: three columns, all leaning away from your goal. Play cards to bring them over. When all three are fully with you, the fourth column — the Spectrum, the recorder that has been watching the whole game — seals your color: pure White, or pure Black. Every play is one click. Par is 10.

Choose your fruition. The two runs are mirror images of each other (Fact 3) — the math is the same, the journey is opposite.

clicks 0 par 10 best —
balance — starts at −7, seals at +7

Columns fully with you are safe — only half-done work can slip back (Fact 2’s grey is a fragile place). The Spectrum column never moves: it is the ledger. It seals at the end.

Balance notes — how this game was tuned (the math)

The deck was tuned by simulating 20,000 games of perfect play. Result: median 10 clicks (mean 10.6), the middle 80% of runs between 6 and 16 clicks, and zero unwinnable games. The random part of each play is worth between 0.75 and 1.5 clicks toward the target — never more than 2 — so skill, not luck, carries a run. Card odds: Coin 3-in-10, Sift 2, Flood 2, Slip 2, Stir 1. Column weights 1, 2, 4 are the model’s own; par 10 is the simulated median under perfect play — beating it means outplaying the deck, not the house.

3.2 · What the weights and parameters do, and why

Why 1, 2, 4, 8. The four columns carry the powers of two. This is not decoration: with distinct powers of two, no subset can split evenly (1+2+4 ≠ 8 on either side), so the perfect balance Δ = 0 has exactly one owner — the all-mixed state. No accidental forgeries of grey are possible; every signed sum is a checkable fingerprint of its choices. Where fingerprints do collide (Fact 5), the histogram below shows it openly.

The gate ε. When a real color enters the machine, each of its four channel signals must be judged: loaded toward light, loaded toward ink, or balanced? The gate ε is how far from neutral a signal must sit before it counts as loaded. Small ε: fine judgments, few colors counted as balanced. Large ε: more colors collapse into the balanced family. There is no “correct” ε — it is the price you pay for a legible answer, and the slider in §3.7 lets you pay it yourself.

The picture. A ledger has no look — someone must draw it. The instrument draws with one stated rule (§3.7’s conventions note): ladder lightness l = ½ + Δ/30, opponent chroma from the three chromatic columns with the achromatic leak projected out, saturation gain 2·min(l, 1−l)/3, then clamp. Other honest observers could draw other pictures; the ledger does not force the picture.

3.3 · The twelve operators

Each color operator X lives in a commutative ring ℭ over ℝ with a nilpotent generator τ² = 0. It decomposes into two pools: X = val(X) + τ·tr(X), where val is the flow pool (light contributed) and tr is the absorption pool (the nilpotent record of wavelengths absorbed — recording the same absorption twice annihilates the record). The operators stand in a 3 × 4 matrix: three mixing regimes over four spectral channels, magnitudes the powers of two, so every assignment leaves a unique imbalance signature.

The named roles. R, B, G — the additive primaries. C, Y, M — the subtractive primaries. W₁ = R + C, W₂ = B + Y, W₃ = G + M — the metameric whites, each holding a different spectral string. W₄ — the additive white reflex: appears pure white; its zero trace records its additive origin. K — the subtractive black reflex: appears pure black; its zero value records its subtractive origin. Ω — the equilibrium operator: flow and absorption balanced at full magnitude, the full potential spectrum.

3.4 · The assignment under analysis

An assignment selects one operator from each column — one regime per channel — giving 3⁴ = 81 possibilities. Operators combine by component-wise addition; the total X = V + τ·T carries the flow V, the absorption T, and the imbalance Δ = V − T. Row I contributes +M to Δ, Row II −M, Row III 0.

3.6 · Color ⇄ tensor: the observer’s gate

Give the instrument a color. It decomposes the color into the four channel signals — red–cyan, blue–yellow, green–magenta (the chromatic columns) and the reflex (luminance) — then quantizes each onto its regime: Row I if the signal exceeds the gate, Row II if it falls below −ε, Row III (the mixed regime) inside the gate. The gate ε is the spectator’s fare: the compression that trades the continuum for the legible 40 / 40 / 1 split (Ch. IX: “the perfect split is purchased by a decision”).

Presets include the equilibrium grey (→ the witness) and pure white — watch how white enters the reflex column while its channels stay neutral: the tensor distinguishes two whites, reflex and additive, that no observer can tell apart.

3.7 · The ring calculator: addition and τ² = 0

Operators add pool-wise and multiply as (v₁ + τt₁)(v₂ + τt₂) = v₁v₂ + τ(v₁t₂ + t₁v₂) — the τ² term is annihilated. Multiply two pure absorptions and the product is zero: recording the same kind of history twice destroys the record. That nilpotency is the ledger’s law — Fact 6 of part 1, in symbols.

Metamerism. W₁, W₂, W₃ and Ω render the identical equilibrium grey yet carry different spectral histories — “different spectral distributions appear as the same color because the observer samples only a finite projection of the continuum.” The projection here is lossless at the level of the 81 assignments (all render distinctly) and lossy exactly where the monograph says it must be: at the cells, and in the shared Δ values (the histogram’s collisions — the price of the powers of two).

Additive–subtractive duality. The all-additive assignment (R+B+G+W₄) renders pure white; the all-subtractive (C+Y+M+K) pure black; and the map that swaps Row I ↔ Row II in every column negates Δ, pairing the 80 non-equilibrium assignments into 40 bright/dark pairs. The duality is an involution of the grammar — Fact 3.

The neutral axis as equilibrium. The witness is not a grey among greys: it is the state where every channel’s flow and absorption cancel exactly — “the grey that underlies every graphic program’s color picker… the ladder is the carry that makes ascent possible; the grey is the carry that makes color possible.” The assignment panel above places every assignment on that ladder — Fact 4.

The instrument’s conventions (how colors are rendered and quantized)

The monograph defines the algebra — the ring, the twelve operators, the mixing rule, Δ, the partition. To make it executable, this instrument fixes three documented conventions:

Cell identity colors. Row I/II cells render their exact primaries (R, B, G, W₄; C, Y, M, K). All four Row III cells render the same equilibrium grey — metamerism by construction.

Assignment rendering (the observer’s projection). With row signs sⱼ ∈ {+1,−1,0} and Δ = Σ sⱼ·Mⱼ: the ladder luminance l = ½ + Δ/30; the opponent chroma (a−b−g, −a−b+g, −a+b−g) from the three chromatic columns, projected to zero achromatic leakage; saturation gain 2·min(l,1−l)/3 — chroma vanishes at the ladder’s black and white ends; then clamp. All 81 assignments render distinctly under this rule.

The gate. A color decomposes as a = r − (g+b)/2, b = bl − (r+g)/2, g = gr − (r+bl)/2, l = (r+g+bl)/3; each chromatic axis quantizes to Row I / III / II against ±ε; the reflex column takes the luminance residual, normalized, against the same ε. The nearest rendered assignment (exact search over all 81) is reported alongside — when gate and projection disagree, your color sits between two ledgers.

4 · Where it breaks

A model earns trust not by its claims but by its failures. The model built here does not compete with real colorimetry — not yet; that is development room, not a verdict. Two different kinds of failure live on this page: the model’s own (a research map), and the computer representation’s (open questions about the gaps in our ordinary understanding of color — the gaps that make metamerism so hard to think about).

4.1 · Where the model fails — the research map

The balance is not brightness

Δ counts bookkeeping weight, not lightness. Green’s column weighs four times red’s for a grammatical reason (the powers of two), not a perceptual one.

Fixing it needs: magnitudes re-derived from measured perception — a fitted model, not an axiom.

The fourth column has no receiver

Human vision delivers three cone signals. The model’s recorder column is bookkeeping, not biology — no eye reports it.

Fixing it needs: a physical or physiological candidate for a fourth channel, or an honest demotion of the recorder to pure convention.

The number collides

Five states share the balance +3 (Fact 5). Any tool that gates on balance alone is blind to how the color was made.

Fixing it needs: finer invariants than a single signed sum — the collision fibers are exactly the unstudied structure.

Mixing is straight-line

Operators add component-wise. Real inks and lights do not: saturation, adaptation, pigment interactions — all absent.

Fixing it needs: the ledger sums separated explicitly from physical mixing models.

Eighty-one states cannot name every color

The grid is deliberately discrete. Most real colors have no home in it — only their quantized shadows do.

Fixing it needs: the continuous ring — the monograph itself calls the discrete split “the fare that discreteness pays to the continuum.”

No wavelengths anywhere

The columns are opponent directions (red-vs-cyan…), not spectral bands. Nothing here touches an actual spectrum.

Fixing it needs: a spectral version — real reflectance curves per operator, the columns rebuilt from physics.

The picture is a convention

Everything you see drawn is one stated observer rule (§3.2). Another honest observer could draw the same ledgers differently.

Fixing it needs: either a canonical observer derived from the algebra, or the honesty kept loud — the instrument states its rule, and so should any successor.

4.2 · Where the computer representation fails — open questions

The pixel erases history

An RGB triplet stores appearance. At the moment of storage, how the color was made is gone — irreversibly. Every metamer collapses into one number, and the difference dies there.

Pickers teach the wrong grey

Every color tool shows grey as one swatch. But balance is a family (Facts 4 and 5) — the UI flattens it, and we inherit the flattening as intuition.

Screens live inside metamerism

RGB white is a legal forgery of sunlight: three phosphors faking a continuum. The gap between the discrete sum and the spectrum is where all the trouble lives — for this model and for your display alike.

Files keep no provenance

No mainstream color format carries how, only what. A paint program cannot tell you which of the five +3-states you painted — it never wrote it down.

The language gap

We say “same color” for same-looking and same-made. The difference is unspeakable in ordinary words — so it is hard to think, so metamerism keeps surprising us. The understanding gap is baked into the vocabulary.

The open question. What would a color representation look like that ordinary intuition reads as made-of rather than looks-like? Carrying the books — a color that remembers — is one answer, and this page is its proof of concept. Where that answer breaks is the list above; that list is the research map. The gaps in our ordinary understanding of color are not embarrassments. They are the subject.
